The 5-minute checklist

  1. Revenue growing? Look for consistent year-over-year growth over 3–5 years.
  2. Actually profitable — or on a credible path? Check operating margin and free cash flow, not just headlines.
  3. Reasonable debt? Debt-to-equity that's low for its industry means survival room in downturns.
  4. A moat? Something competitors can't easily copy: network effects, switching costs, brand, scale, patents.
  5. Price vs. value? A great company at an extreme valuation can still be a poor decade-long hold.
  6. Would I still believe this in 10 years? If the answer depends on next quarter, it's not a long-term thesis.

Keep learning

Free, reputable places to keep learning. Start with primary sources — the company's own filings — before anyone's opinion of them.

Primary sources & references

  • SEC EDGAR — every US public company's actual filings: 10-K annual reports, 10-Q quarterlies. The numbers behind every metric above live here.
  • Company investor relations pages — search "[company name] investor relations" for earnings calls, presentations, and annual letters in plain language.
  • Investopedia — solid definitions and tutorials for every term in the glossary.
  • Bogleheads — a community built around low-cost index investing; excellent wiki on asset allocation and Roth vs Traditional.

Books worth your time

  • One Up On Wall Street — Peter Lynch. The classic case for investing in what you know from your own life and work. Directly matches this site's "personal conviction" idea.
  • The Intelligent Investor — Benjamin Graham. The value-investing bible: margin of safety, Mr. Market, and why temperament beats intellect.
  • A Random Walk Down Wall Street — Burton Malkiel. The honest counter-argument: why beating the market is so hard, and why index funds are the default for most people.

Read all three and you'll hold both sides of the argument in your head — which is exactly where a good investor lives.

Roth IRA & account basics

Where you invest matters almost as much as what you buy, because taxes compound too. This is general education, not tax advice — check irs.gov for current-year figures.

The three account types

Roth IRA

What's going on here? You put in money you've already paid tax on. Inside the account, investments grow and qualified withdrawals in retirement are tax-free.

Why should I care? Decades of tax-free growth favor young investors expecting higher future income — and you can withdraw your contributions (not earnings) anytime without penalty, which makes it forgiving to start.

Traditional IRA / 401(k)

What's going on here? Contributions often reduce your taxable income now; you pay tax when you withdraw in retirement.

Why should I care? The deduction is worth most when your tax rate is highest — but withdrawing before 59½ usually brings penalties, so this money is genuinely long-term locked.

Taxable brokerage

What's going on here? No special tax treatment — you owe tax on dividends and on gains in the years you realize them.

Why should I care? You can sell anytime with no penalties, and gains on holdings kept 1+ year get lower long-term capital-gains rates. Best used as overflow after tax-advantaged accounts are full.

2026 contribution limits (verify at irs.gov)

  • IRA (Roth or Traditional combined): $7,500 if under 50; $8,600 if 50+ (includes catch-up). Confirm the current year's figure at irs.gov before contributing.
  • 401(k) employee contribution: $24,500 if under 50; higher with catch-up if 50+. Employer plans vary — check your plan documents.
  • Roth IRA income limits: eligibility phases out above certain incomes (check irs.gov for this year's thresholds); high earners sometimes use the "backdoor Roth" strategy — research it before attempting.

Why index funds fit tax-advantaged accounts so well

  1. They rarely force taxable events on you. Broad index funds trade infrequently, so little of your return leaks to taxes each year — and inside a Roth/Traditional account, it wouldn't matter anyway.
  2. Your conviction stocks can live anywhere, but long holds (1+ year) in a taxable account get favorable long-term capital-gains rates — another reason to buy and hold.
  3. Common beginner structure: max out tax-advantaged accounts with index funds first, then hold a smaller conviction-stock slice wherever fits your plan. This mirrors the allocation bands on the tab.

Tip: many employers match 401(k) contributions — that's an instant return no stock can promise. Contribute at least enough to capture the full match before investing elsewhere.
